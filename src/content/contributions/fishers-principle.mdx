## Why it matters

The principle is a clean example of frequency-dependent selection. It explains an equilibrium not by equal survival or social symmetry, but by the higher expected reproductive return of producing the rarer sex.

## Intuition

Every sexually produced offspring has one genetic father and one genetic mother. If males are rare, the average male has more reproductive opportunities, so parents genetically inclined to produce males gain an advantage. The reverse holds when females are rare.

## Prerequisites

The relevant quantity is parental expenditure, not necessarily the number of births. Costs can differ between male and female offspring, and the argument concerns the population at the end of parental investment.

## Formal statement

If the expected reproductive return per unit cost of sons exceeds that of daughters, selection favors increased allocation to sons, and vice versa. At equilibrium the marginal returns per unit parental expenditure are equal.

## Worked example

If sons cost twice as much to raise as daughters, equal total expenditure could produce one son for every two daughters. A numerical 1:1 sex ratio follows only when costs are equal.

## History and attribution

Earlier thinkers, including Darwin, noticed the puzzle of sex ratios. Fisher’s 1930 treatment gave the argument its influential frequency-dependent and parental-expenditure form.

## Modern use

The principle is a starting point for sex-allocation theory in organisms with unequal costs, local mate competition, inbreeding, environmental sex determination, and changing reproductive values.

## Limits and debates

The simple equilibrium assumes random mating at the relevant scale, symmetric genetic transmission, and no special population structure. Biased sex ratios need not contradict the principle; they often identify which assumptions or cost symmetries do not hold.
