## Why it matters

The theorem is one of population genetics’ most famous and most frequently oversimplified statements. It isolates a precise contribution of natural selection to change in mean fitness rather than promising that total fitness must always rise.

## Intuition

If heritable differences in reproductive success exist, selection changes allele frequencies. The additive genetic variance in fitness measures the portion of fitness differences predictably associated with transmitted alleles. That component determines an instantaneous selective increase.

## Prerequisites

“Fitness” must be defined in Fisher’s population-genetic sense, and additive variance uses average effects of alleles—not the total phenotypic variance of reproductive success.

## Formal statement

In one common modern phrasing, the rate of increase in mean fitness attributable to natural selection equals the additive genetic variance in fitness, with scaling determined by whether time and fitness are discrete or continuous.

$$\Delta \bar w\big|_{selection}\propto V_A(w).$$

## Worked example

When alleles differ predictably in reproductive success, the selected allele-frequency change raises the population’s mean fitness component. Simultaneous environmental deterioration or frequency-dependent changes can offset that increase in the total observed mean.

## History and attribution

Fisher presented the theorem in 1930 and compared its status to a conservation law. Sewall Wright and others read it as a broad monotonicity claim; later scholarship emphasized Fisher’s narrower separation of selection from changes in the environment, including the genetic environment.

## Modern use

The theorem organizes discussions of adaptation, breeding value, allele-frequency change, and the decomposition of evolutionary change. The so-called paradox of low fitness heritability despite continuing evolution is a later discussion around those distinctions, not a second theorem by Fisher. The theorem also motivates links to quantitative-genetic versions of selection theory.

## Limits and debates

The slogan “natural selection always increases mean fitness” is misleading. Frequency dependence, changing environments, linkage, non-additive effects, and the definition of average effects matter. Much historical disagreement concerned interpretation rather than algebra alone.
