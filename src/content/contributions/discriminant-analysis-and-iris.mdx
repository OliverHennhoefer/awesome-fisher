## Why it matters

The discriminant is an early, influential bridge between statistics and supervised learning. It turns several measurements into a single direction chosen to separate labeled groups.

## Intuition

A useful projection places group centers far apart while keeping each projected group tight. Fisher’s criterion measures separation as a ratio, not merely as raw distance.

## Prerequisites

Let $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ be class means and $S_W$ the pooled within-class scatter matrix. Covariance must be estimable and the labels meaningful.

## Formal statement

For projection vector $w$, maximize

$$J(w)=\frac{[w^T(\mu_1-\mu_2)]^2}{w^TS_Ww}.$$

When $S_W$ is invertible, the maximizing direction is proportional to $S_W^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_2)$.

## Worked example

Edgar Anderson measured sepal and petal dimensions for three iris species. A discriminant heavily weighting petal measurements separates *Iris setosa* especially clearly; the remaining two species overlap more.

## History and attribution

Fisher’s 1936 paper used Anderson’s measurements to demonstrate the method. Calling the dataset simply “Fisher’s iris data” misattributes the collection: Anderson assembled and published the botanical data, while Fisher supplied the famous discriminant analysis.

## Modern use

Linear discriminant analysis remains a classifier, dimension-reduction method, and baseline for high-dimensional extensions. Under multivariate normal class models with common covariance, it also has a probabilistic classification interpretation.

## Limits and debates

The classical method can fail when covariance is singular, groups are strongly non-normal, boundaries are nonlinear, or labels encode biased historical categories. The iris dataset is pedagogically convenient but too small and tidy to stand in for modern classification practice.
