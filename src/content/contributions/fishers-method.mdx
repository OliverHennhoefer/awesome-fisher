## Why it matters

Several studies can each provide modest evidence in the same direction without any one crossing a conventional threshold. Fisher’s method supplies a compact global test of whether all corresponding null hypotheses could simultaneously hold.

## Intuition

Under a valid continuous null, a p-value is uniform on $(0,1)$. Very small p-values make $-\log p$ large. Adding these transformed values accumulates evidence without reducing the collection to “significant” versus “not significant.”

## Prerequisites

The classical reference distribution requires mutually independent p-values, each valid for its own null hypothesis. The method tests the intersection null that every component null is true.

## Formal statement

For $k$ independent p-values,

$$X=-2\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log p_i.$$

Under the global null, $X\sim\chi^2_{2k}$. Small p-values—through their logarithms—contribute especially strongly.

## Worked example

For p-values 0.01, 0.04, 0.03, and 0.20, the statistic is about 25.880 on 8 degrees of freedom, giving a combined p-value near 0.0011. The result rejects the global null but does not identify which component alternatives are true.

## History and attribution

Fisher introduced the method in the first edition of *Statistical Methods for Research Workers*. Weighted and generalized Fisher methods are later variants. Under dependence, Brown-type approximations modify the null calibration rather than the basic product idea; meta-analysis also offers alternatives tailored to direction and effect size.

## Modern use

The method appears in meta-analysis, genomics, signal detection, and omnibus testing. Its appeal is simplicity: only valid component p-values are needed.

## Limits and debates

Correlation among component tests changes the null distribution, sometimes drastically. The method ignores effect direction and magnitude, and a single tiny p-value can dominate. When comparable effect estimates exist, a model that combines those estimates can answer a more informative question.
