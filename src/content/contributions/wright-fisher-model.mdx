## Why it matters

Even alleles with identical fitness change frequency in a finite population because reproduction is a sampling process. Wright–Fisher is the baseline model for quantifying that genetic drift and for adding mutation, selection, migration, or population structure.

## Intuition

Form the next generation by drawing gene copies independently from the current generation’s gene pool. A frequency of one half is only the expected outcome; the realized number fluctuates. Repeating the sampling eventually loses or fixes a neutral allele.

## Prerequisites

In a diploid population of constant size $N$, there are $2N$ copies at an autosomal locus. Let $p_t$ be the frequency of allele $A$ in generation $t$.

## Formal statement

Under the neutral biallelic model,

$$X_{t+1}\mid p_t\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(2N,p_t),
\qquad p_{t+1}=\frac{X_{t+1}}{2N}.$$

Thus $\mathbb E(p_{t+1}\mid p_t)=p_t$ and $\operatorname{Var}(p_{t+1}\mid p_t)=p_t(1-p_t)/(2N)$. The Wright–Fisher diffusion is a continuous-time, large-population approximation, not the original discrete chain.

## Worked example

With $N=50$ and $p_t=0.4$, the next generation contains $X_{t+1}\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(100,0.4)$ copies. Its expected frequency remains $0.4$, with standard deviation $\sqrt{0.4(0.6)/100}\approx0.049$.

## History and attribution

Fisher analyzed binomial sampling and finite-population variation in the 1920s, while Wright developed parallel and more explicit random-drift models. Historical scholarship shows that the textbook “Wright–Fisher model” is a later synthesis rather than a single jointly authored formula. Both names belong; their broader views of evolution differed sharply.

## Modern use

The model underlies population-genetic likelihoods, diffusion theory, fixation probabilities, coalescent approximations, genetic association null models, and simulations. Multiallelic, multilocus, selected, mutating, migrating, and structured versions modify its transition rule.

## Limits and debates

Real populations overlap in generations, vary in size, have unequal reproductive success, and are spatially structured. Independent sampling can therefore misstate drift. Effective population size is often introduced to match some variance property, but no single effective size captures every departure from the model.
