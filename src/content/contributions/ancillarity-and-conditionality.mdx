## Why it matters

Two experiments can produce the same likelihood but have different precision because of how the data were generated. Ancillary information can identify which experiment effectively occurred and therefore which repeated-sampling comparison is relevant.

## Intuition

Imagine first tossing a fair coin to choose between a precise and an imprecise measuring instrument. The coin result says nothing about the unknown quantity, but once observed it says a great deal about the uncertainty of the measurement. Conditioning on that result avoids averaging precision across an experiment that did not happen.

## Prerequisites

For data $X\sim P_\theta$, a statistic $A(X)$ is ancillary when its sampling distribution does not depend on $\theta$. A statistic $T$ is sufficient when the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T$ does not depend on $\theta$; the two concepts play different roles.

## Formal statement

Ancillarity means

$$P_\theta\{A(X)\in B\}=P\{A(X)\in B\}
\quad\text{for every }\theta.$$

Conditional inference evaluates the distribution of an inferential statistic $T$ given $A=a$, rather than its unconditional distribution. Basu’s later theorem shows that a boundedly complete sufficient statistic is independent of every ancillary statistic under its assumptions.

## Worked example

If an instrument indicator $A$ is chosen with probability $1/2$ independently of $\theta$, and $X\mid A=a\sim N(\theta,\sigma_a^2)$, then $A$ is ancillary. After observing $A=1$, a confidence interval should use $\sigma_1$ rather than average over both $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$.

## History and attribution

Fisher introduced the term in 1934, using problems including the Nile data to argue that relevant information can remain outside a reduced sufficient statistic. The resulting disagreement with Neyman is sometimes called the Neyman–Fisher paradox; it is a foundational example, not a distinct probability law. Fisher later treated conditionality as part of a connected program involving likelihood and fiducial inference. The strong conditionality principle and modern conditional-likelihood methods are later formalizations, not a single theorem authored only by Fisher.

## Modern use

Ancillary conditioning informs exact inference, higher-order likelihood approximations, conditional logistic regression, matched studies, and assessments of post-experiment precision. Approximate ancillaries are important when exact ones do not exist.

## Limits and debates

Ancillaries may be nonunique, unavailable, or hard to recognize, and different choices can lead to different conditional analyses. How strongly one ought to condition is foundationally disputed. An ancillary statistic is not “irrelevant data”; it is parameter-free data that may be highly relevant to precision.
