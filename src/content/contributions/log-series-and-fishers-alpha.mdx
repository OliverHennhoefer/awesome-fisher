## Why it matters

Ecological samples contain many rare species and a few common ones. The log-series model gave an early quantitative account of that pattern and Fisher’s alpha supplied a diversity measure less directly tied to sample size than raw species count.

## Intuition

For every species seen twice, a log-series community predicts more species seen once and fewer seen three, four, or more times. One parameter controls the overall richness scale; another governs how quickly frequencies decline.

## Prerequisites

Let $a_n$ be the expected number of species represented by exactly $n$ sampled individuals. Let $S=\sum_n a_n$ be observed richness and $N=\sum_n n a_n$ total individuals.

## Formal statement

The model sets

$$a_n=\frac{\alpha x^n}{n},\qquad 0<x<1,$$

so $N=\alpha x/(1-x)$ and $S=-\alpha\log(1-x)$. Eliminating $x$ gives the estimating relation

$$S=\alpha\log\left(1+\frac{N}{\alpha}\right).$$

The fitted $\alpha$ is Fisher’s alpha.

## Worked example

If a sample contains $N=100$ individuals from $S=30$ species, solve $30=\alpha\log(1+100/\alpha)$. The solution is approximately $\alpha=14.5$. A second sample should only be compared through alpha if the model and sampling assumptions are credible for both.

## History and attribution

The 1943 paper was joint work. Corbet supplied Malayan butterfly data, Williams supplied British moth data, and Fisher developed the mathematical relation and fitted it. Calling the entire result only “Fisher’s” erases the empirical collaboration; “Fisher–Corbet–Williams log series” makes that structure visible.

## Modern use

Fisher’s alpha remains a biodiversity index and the log series remains a baseline species-abundance model. The apparent coexistence of many rare species—sometimes loosely called Fisher’s paradox of species abundance—is a question motivated by the pattern, not a separate formal Fisher theorem. The paper also helped establish quantitative macroecology and species-accumulation analysis.

## Limits and debates

Alpha is model-dependent, not a universal property of a community. Habitat heterogeneity, incomplete detection, spatial aggregation, and sampling design can change it. Other abundance models may fit better, and equal alpha values do not imply identical ecological structure.
