## Why it matters

Many statistical questions compare a variation explained by a model with unexplained variation. Turning that comparison into a ratio removes the common variance scale, and the F distribution supplies a reference law for judging whether the ratio is unusually large.

## Intuition

Two independent variance estimates fluctuate even when they estimate the same quantity. Their ratio should hover near one, but its right tail is asymmetric because a small denominator can make the ratio very large. Degrees of freedom determine how variable each estimate is.

## Prerequisites

Let $U\sim\chi^2_{d_1}$ and $V\sim\chi^2_{d_2}$ be independent. The chi-square variables often arise as residual sums of squares divided by a common normal-error variance.

## Formal statement

The scaled ratio

$$F=\frac{U/d_1}{V/d_2}$$

has the central $F_{d_1,d_2}$ distribution. Under alternatives, a noncentral chi-square numerator produces a noncentral F distribution; allowing both components to be noncentral gives a later doubly noncentral extension. Fisher’s earlier $z$ distribution used $z=\tfrac12\log F$ and is not the correlation $z$ transformation.

## Worked example

If a model comparison yields a mean square of $24$ on $3$ degrees of freedom and an error mean square of $4$ on $20$ degrees of freedom, the variance ratio is $F=24/4=6$. Its reference distribution is $F_{3,20}$, not an F distribution with the degrees of freedom reversed.

## History and attribution

Fisher derived the distribution in 1924 while unifying several sampling problems. That work also treated the historically named Fisher B distribution, a related noncentral radial sampling law rather than another modern synonym for F. Snedecor chose the letter F in Fisher’s honor and developed influential tables and teaching conventions, which is why “Fisher–Snedecor distribution” is also used. Cochran’s later theorem—occasionally labeled Fisher–Cochran—clarifies the independent chi-square decompositions behind many F tests; it is not a separate Fisher theorem in this index.

## Modern use

F statistics appear in analysis of variance, nested linear-model tests, overall regression tests, variance-component work, and power calculations through noncentral F laws. Software generally reports the statistic, two degrees of freedom, and a tail probability.

## Limits and debates

Exact classical F laws require the stated independence and normal-error structure. Heteroskedasticity, dependence, data-driven model selection, or a denominator near zero can invalidate the reference distribution. A significant F test says that a modeled contrast exists; it does not identify which scientific explanation is correct.
