## Why it matters

Changing one factor at a time cannot reveal interactions efficiently. A factorial design studies factors jointly, often extracting several answers from the same experimental runs.

## Intuition

With fertilizer and irrigation each at two levels, the four combinations reveal two main effects and whether the effect of fertilizer changes with irrigation. Blocking may force one high-order contrast to be mixed with block differences; deliberate confounding chooses that sacrifice in advance.

## Prerequisites

Factors must have operationally meaningful levels, and the experimental unit and assignment scheme must be clear. “Interaction” describes non-additivity on the chosen response scale.

## Formal statement

For a $2^2$ design, a simple model is

$$Y=\mu+\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma AB+\varepsilon.$$

Orthogonal assignments let the contrasts for $A$, $B$, and $AB$ be estimated independently in a balanced design.

## Worked example

Run all four fertilizer–irrigation combinations on plots within each replicate. If fertilizer helps only under high irrigation, the interaction contrast is large; averaging over irrigation would hide the scientifically important dependency.

## History and attribution

Fisher argued for factorial arrangements against one-factor-at-a-time experimentation and developed their analysis at Rothamsted. Yates made major contributions to computational methods, confounding schemes, and the practical design tradition.

## Modern use

Factorial logic drives industrial experiments, A/B/n tests, agricultural trials, screening designs, and response-surface methods. Fractional factorials trade selected aliases for fewer runs.

## Limits and debates

Large full factorials grow exponentially. High-order interactions are difficult to estimate and explain, while fractional designs depend on assumptions about which effects are negligible. An interaction is scale-dependent and should not be interpreted without plotting fitted responses.
