## Why it matters

Why are many harmful mutations recessive while wild-type alleles appear dominant? Fisher argued that dominance itself could evolve through selection at other loci, turning a descriptive genetic pattern into a problem about indirect selection.

## Intuition

A modifier that makes a heterozygote resemble the fitter homozygote hides part of a deleterious mutation’s effect. Even if the advantage is small, recurrent mutations continually recreate heterozygotes on which the modifier can be favored.

## Prerequisites

Consider a deleterious allele with selection coefficient $s$ and dominance coefficient $h$, so the heterozygote has fitness approximately $1-hs$. A modifier allele changes $h$ slightly without being the focal deleterious allele itself.

## Formal statement

If a modifier changes dominance from $h$ to $h-\delta$, it improves heterozygote fitness by approximately $s\delta$. Its population-level selection coefficient is smaller because that benefit is expressed only in the relevant genotypes and is altered by linkage and mutation–selection balance.

## Worked example

With $s=0.2$, reducing $h$ from $0.10$ to $0.08$ raises heterozygote fitness from $0.98$ to $0.984$, a direct difference of $0.004$ when the modifier and deleterious allele meet. The modifier’s average advantage is much smaller when such heterozygotes are rare.

## History and attribution

Fisher presented the modifier account in 1928 and expanded it in 1930. Wright argued that deleterious alleles are too rare for selection on dominance modifiers to be generally effective and favored physiological explanations. Later theory found conditions under which modifier selection can work while rejecting it as a universal account.

## Modern use

The debate helped establish modifier theory and continues to inform models of dominance, canalization, robustness, and genetic-background effects. It is also a case study in how small indirect selection coefficients should be evaluated.

## Limits and debates

The hypothesis does not explain every form of dominance and is difficult to distinguish empirically from biochemical and network-based mechanisms. Results depend on mutation rates, linkage, epistasis, and standing variation. The historical Fisher–Wright dispute should be presented as a substantive scientific disagreement, not a settled victory for either side.
