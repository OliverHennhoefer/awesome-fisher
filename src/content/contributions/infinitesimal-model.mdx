## Why it matters

The model helped reconcile two early schools: Mendelians studying discrete genes and biometricians studying continuous traits. It became a foundation of quantitative genetics, animal breeding, and modern polygenic prediction.

## Intuition

One gene can have discrete effects. Add the small contributions of very many segregating genes plus environmental variation, and offspring resemble their relatives on a nearly continuous, bell-shaped scale.

## Prerequisites

The classical approximation assumes many loci with small individual effects, sufficiently weak linkage or recombination, and a trait assembled from additive and possibly modest non-additive components.

## Formal statement

As the number of contributing loci grows while individual effects shrink, the segregation component of offspring phenotype approaches a normal distribution. Conditional on parental values, its variance becomes approximately stable even under selection over short timescales.

## Worked example

Height is not inherited as a single averaged parental number. Many alleles segregate, so siblings differ around a family expectation. The aggregate of many small contributions produces both resemblance and within-family variation.

## History and attribution

Fisher’s 1918 paper decomposed correlations between relatives under Mendelian inheritance and demonstrated that continuous biometric variation did not contradict discrete inheritance.

## Modern use

The approximation supports the breeder’s equation, mixed-model prediction, genomic relatedness methods, and theoretical work on polygenic adaptation.

## Limits and debates

Major-effect loci, strong linkage, epistasis, assortative mating, population structure, and long-term selection can violate simple versions. “Infinitesimal” is an approximation about aggregate inheritance, not a claim that every causal effect is literally infinitesimal.
