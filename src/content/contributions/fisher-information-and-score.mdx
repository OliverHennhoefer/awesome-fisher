## Why it matters

Fisher information connects model sensitivity, estimator precision, hypothesis tests, and the geometry of probability models. It explains why some experiments can estimate a parameter much more precisely than others even with the same sample size.

## Intuition

The score asks which way—and how strongly—the log-likelihood slopes at a proposed parameter. Information asks how much that slope fluctuates when data are repeatedly generated from the model. A sharply curved likelihood makes nearby parameter values easier to tell apart than a flat one.

## Prerequisites

Let $\ell(\theta;X)$ be a differentiable log-likelihood. Standard identities require regularity conditions that permit differentiation under the integral and keep the support from changing with $\theta$.

## Formal statement

The score is $U(\theta)=\partial\ell(\theta;X)/\partial\theta$. Under regularity conditions, $\mathbb E_\theta[U(\theta)]=0$ and

$$I(\theta)=\mathbb E_\theta[U(\theta)^2]
=-\mathbb E_\theta\left[\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\theta^2}\right].$$

For several parameters, the outer product becomes an information matrix.

## Worked example

For one Bernoulli observation, $U(p)=X/p-(1-X)/(1-p)$ and $I(p)=1/[p(1-p)]$. For $n$ independent observations, information adds: $I_n(p)=n/[p(1-p)]$. This predicts the familiar variance scale $p(1-p)/n$.

## History and attribution

Fisher introduced “information” as an inferential quantity and integrated it with likelihood, sufficiency, and efficient estimation. The modern information matrix, scoring algorithms, information geometry, and lower-bound theory were developed through a broader lineage.

## Modern use

Information controls standard-error approximations, optimal design, score tests, natural-gradient methods, and asymptotic likelihood theory. Observed and expected information are related but not identical finite-sample quantities.

## Limits and debates

The clean identities can fail at boundaries, in mixture models, when support depends on the parameter, or under misspecification. High information inside a wrong model does not make an estimate scientifically correct; it can produce very precise answers to the wrong question.
