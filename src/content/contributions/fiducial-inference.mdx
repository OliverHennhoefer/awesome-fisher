## Why it matters

Fiducial inference is historically important because it reveals Fisher’s dissatisfaction with both long-run confidence statements and subjective prior distributions. Modern generalized fiducial and confidence-distribution methods revisit parts of the ambition.

## Intuition

Start with a data-generating equation containing a known random quantity and an unknown parameter. After observing the data, Fisher proposed holding the data fixed and transferring the randomness to the parameter by inversion—without calling the result a Bayesian posterior.

## Prerequisites

The simplest examples use a pivotal quantity whose distribution does not depend on unknown parameters. More complex problems make the choice of data-generating equation consequential.

## Formal statement

If $X=\theta+Z$ with $Z\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$ and known $\sigma$, observing $x$ permits the inversion $\theta=x-Z$. The resulting fiducial distribution is $N(x,\sigma^2)$.

## Worked example

The normal-location fiducial interval numerically matches the usual confidence interval and the Bayesian posterior under a flat prior. That agreement is special: in multiparameter problems, different inversions can produce incompatible answers.

## History and attribution

Fisher introduced fiducial probability around 1930 and defended it for decades. Neyman, Pearson, and later statisticians challenged its probability interpretation and lack of a unique construction.

## Modern use

Classical fiducial language is uncommon in routine practice, but related ideas survive in exact confidence distributions, inferential models, generalized fiducial inference, and structural approaches.

## Limits and debates

There is no general fiducial recipe that is invariant to every equivalent representation. The method can conceal prior-like choices inside an inversion. Its most defensible examples often coincide with established confidence or Bayesian procedures, leaving the distinct interpretation contested.
