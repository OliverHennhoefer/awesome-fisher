## Why it matters

Raw correlations live between −1 and 1 and have a skewed, parameter-dependent sampling distribution. The transformation makes confidence intervals and comparisons easier on an unbounded scale.

## Intuition

Near ±1, a small change in correlation means something different from the same numerical change near 0. The inverse hyperbolic tangent stretches the ends of the interval so sampling variability becomes approximately even.

## Prerequisites

The classic approximation assumes independent observations from a bivariate normal population. Let $r$ be the sample correlation and $\rho$ the population correlation.

## Formal statement

Define

$$z=\operatorname{arctanh}(r)=\frac12\log\left(\frac{1+r}{1-r}\right).$$

Then $z$ is approximately normal with mean $\operatorname{arctanh}(\rho)$ and standard error $1/\sqrt{n-3}$.

## Worked example

With $r=0.60$ and $n=50$, $z\approx0.693$ and the standard error is about 0.146. Form an interval on the z-scale, then apply $\tanh$ to move the endpoints back to the correlation scale.

## History and attribution

Fisher’s 1921 paper developed the small-sample approximation and transformation. It became part of his broader program of deriving usable sampling distributions for common statistics.

## Modern use

The transformation remains common in correlation confidence intervals, tests comparing independent correlations, and meta-analysis of correlations.

## Limits and debates

The normal approximation can be poor with very small samples, non-normal data, dependence, or correlations near the boundary. It does not repair biased sampling, measurement error, nonlinear association, or the familiar fact that correlation does not establish causation.
