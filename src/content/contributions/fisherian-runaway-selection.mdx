## Why it matters

The model explains how elaborate displays can evolve beyond what direct survival advantage would predict. Preferences themselves become part of the selective environment.

## Intuition

Suppose some females prefer a longer display trait. Preferred males leave more offspring, and those offspring inherit both display alleles and preference alleles. The resulting genetic association lets preference and display pull one another upward.

## Prerequisites

The mechanism requires heritable variation in both preference and display, non-random mating, and genetic covariance between them. Natural selection can oppose costly exaggeration.

## Formal statement

In modern quantitative-genetic models, the change in trait and preference means depends on their genetic variance–covariance matrix multiplied by selection gradients. A positive covariance creates the feedback direction associated with runaway.

## Worked example

If females preferring longer tails tend to produce sons with longer tails and daughters with the same preference, both sons and daughters gain mating advantages through the prevailing choice pattern. Survival costs eventually may balance that advantage.

## History and attribution

Fisher outlined the process in 1915 and more fully in *The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection*. Later models by Lande, Kirkpatrick, and others made the verbal mechanism mathematically testable.

## Modern use

Runaway is one hypothesis among several for ornaments, courtship signals, and preference coevolution. Empirical work tests genetic covariance, preference functions, survival costs, and phylogenetic predictions.

## Limits and debates

An attractive trait is not automatically evidence for runaway. Direct benefits, sensory bias, species recognition, good-genes processes, and ecological selection can create similar patterns. The mechanism is a dynamic explanation, not a label for every elaborate ornament.
