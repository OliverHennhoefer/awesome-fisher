## Why it matters

Sample moments are easy to calculate but are generally biased for higher population cumulants. k-statistics remove that bias exactly under independent sampling, making them foundational tools for finite-sample moment calculations.

## Intuition

Repeated indices in powers of a sample sum create extra terms that have no population analogue. k-statistics combine power sums so those unwanted coincidences cancel, leaving an expectation equal to the desired cumulant.

## Prerequisites

For independent identically distributed observations $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, let $\bar X$ be the sample mean and $s^2=\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2/(n-1)$ the usual sample variance.

## Formal statement

The first two k-statistics are

$$k_1=\bar X,\qquad
k_2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\bar X)^2.$$

They satisfy $\mathbb E(k_1)=\kappa_1$ and $\mathbb E(k_2)=\kappa_2$, the first two population cumulants. Higher $k_r$ are polynomial combinations of sample power sums with $\mathbb E(k_r)=\kappa_r$ when the required moments exist.

## Worked example

For data $1,2,3$, $k_1=2$. The squared deviations sum to $2$, so $k_2=2/(3-1)=1$. Dividing by $n$ instead would give $2/3$, the biased empirical second central moment.

## History and attribution

Fisher developed the general theory in his 1929 work on moments and product moments of sampling distributions. The letter $k$ reflects cumulants, historically also called semi-invariants. Polykays, which estimate products of cumulants, and partition-lattice treatments are later developments.

## Modern use

k-statistics appear in unbiased skewness and kurtosis estimation, sampling theory, method-of-moments calculations, random-effects work, signal processing, and symbolic statistics. Their combinatorics also connect cumulants with set partitions.

## Limits and debates

Unbiasedness does not guarantee low variance: high-order k-statistics can be extremely unstable and require large samples and finite high moments. Terminology varies across software, where “sample kurtosis” may denote several incompatible corrections.
